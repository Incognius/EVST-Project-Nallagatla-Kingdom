## Slide 5. How the components fit together

**What is on it.** Four boxes, left to right: sources, processing, models and evaluation. Under them are four real covariate maps (rainfall, tree cover, people, road distance).

**Sources.**
- iNaturalist open data for India and Taiwan: the citizen-science records we train on.
- WorldClim 2.1: 19 climate layers plus elevation.
- ESA WorldCover at 10 m: the fractions of 9 land-cover classes (tree, shrub, grass, crop, built-up, bare, water, wetland, mangrove).
- WorldPop 2020 population, and OpenStreetMap roads, towns and reserves.
- Truth data: the Kerala Bird Atlas, the Taiwan survey, the disdat benchmark and GeoLifeCLEF 2024 Europe (88,987 plant survey plots).

**Processing.**
- **Cleaning.** We keep research-grade records identified to species, with location error of 5 km or less. We remove records sitting on institution or country centroids, which are usually a default location rather than a real sighting.
- **The grid.** 1 km cells, each with 34 numbers: **29 habitat layers** (what species respond to) and **5 access layers** (what observers respond to: distance to any road, a major road, a town and a protected area, plus log population).
- **A visit** is one observer on one day. This is the unit of sampling effort.
- **Names.** Species names are matched to the atlas name table in India and to the GBIF backbone in Taiwan.

**Models.** We used a ladder from simple to flexible:
- **GLM:** logistic regression with squared terms, so a species can prefer a middle value.
- **MaxEnt:** the most-used presence-only model.
- **Boosted regression trees (BRT):** many small trees, each correcting the last one's errors.
- **Random forest:** an average of many trees.
- **MLP:** a basic neural network.
- **Patch CNN:** looks at a 9×9 km window of the layers, like an image.

There are also three special models: the effort-aware network, the Bayesian discovery model (slide 15), and an occupancy model that checks the survey truth.

**Evaluation.** The same models are judged several ways: random and spatial-block cross-validation, later citizen-science records, the missing-pair protocol against the survey (slide 11), and the same-pair truth-source test. **Survey-size subsampling** asks how many survey sites a fair test needs (slide 9). Uncertainty comes from a spatial-block bootstrap and Wilcoxon tests.

**The point to land.** Each cell carries both habitat and access, and that entanglement is the whole problem. A single system switch builds identical inputs for Kerala and Taiwan, so the replication ran with exactly the same code.

**Likely question.** *Why one observer per day as a visit?* It is the closest thing presence-only data has to a survey visit. Counting raw records would weight one keen photographer's 200 photos as 200 visits.

## Slide 8. Virtual species: more bias, worse maps, better CV scores (H2 to H4)

**What the experiment is.** We invented 30 species. Each has a bell-shaped (Gaussian) preference for climate, elevation and natural land cover, placed on the real Western Ghats map. So we know exactly where each one lives. We then "recorded" 300 occurrences per species, choosing places in proportion to the real observer-effort surface fitted from iNaturalist. That surface was raised to a power of 0 (no bias), 1 (realistic) or 2 (exaggerated). The design follows Inman et al. (2021).

**Theory you need.**
- **AUC:** pick one place where the species is present and one where it is absent; AUC is how often the model scores the present place higher. 1.0 is perfect and 0.5 is a coin toss.
- **Presence-only models** compare places where the species was recorded (label 1) with **background** places (label 0). With random background, roadside records are compared with random land, so "near a road" looks like habitat.
- **Random cross-validation** hides random records and scores the model on them. The hidden records come from the same biased observers.
- **Bias leakage:** the share of variation in the predicted map that the access layers explain beyond the true suitability. It can only be measured where the truth is known, which is why we need virtual species.
- **Target-group background (TGB):** the background is every place where anyone recorded a species of the same group. Presences and background then both come from places people visited, so the observer bias sits on both sides of the comparison and cancels.

**The three panels.**
- **a.** As bias grows, true AUC falls (0.79 to 0.71) while random-CV AUC rises (0.74 to 0.92). A better CV score here means more bias absorbed, not a better map.
- **b.** Every flexible model leaks more bias than the GLM (Wilcoxon p ≤ 0.003). Boosted trees leak most here. On real European survey plots the CNN does absorb more bias than a simple network (slide 10), so do not say "the CNN is fine".
- **c.** TGB is the truly best correction for 77 to 90% of species. Thinning barely helps. Random CV picks a different correction, bias-covariate conditioning, because that model uses access directly and the biased test records reward it.

**The point to land.** H2 (flexible models absorb bias), H3 (random CV misses it) and H4 (corrections can be ranked, TGB best) all hold where the truth is known.

**Likely question.** *Aren't virtual species artificial?* Yes, which is why the next slides repeat the test on real surveys: random CV picks wrong in four real systems (slide 9), and the CNN result is checked on 88,987 European plots (slide 10). Here they give us the one thing real data cannot: the true map.

**Likely question.** *Why did the CNN do worst here?* These virtual species respond only to point values, so a CNN's 15 km view cannot help. On real European plots (slide 10) it ties a simple network on accuracy but drifts further toward towns.

## Slide 11. Corrected models find the birds citizen science missed (H5)

**The gap.** 52% of Kerala atlas cells have no iNaturalist bird record up to 2020. Where iNaturalist did visit, it recorded only 12% of the species the atlas found.

**The missing-pair protocol (our main evaluation).**
1. Take every cell and species pair that iNaturalist never recorded.
2. The survey tells us which of these pairs actually hold the species.
3. Per species, compute the AUC: does the model rank the pairs where the species really is above the pairs where it is not?

This scores a model exactly where gap-filling claims are made: the places citizen science missed.

**The numbers.**

| | Kerala | Taiwan |
|---|---|---|
| Pairs scored | 170,286 | 58,551 |
| Survey presence (base rate) | 27.5% | 17.0% |
| BRT with target-group background, AUC | 0.758 | 0.820 |
| Gain over random background | +0.022 [0.015, 0.028] | +0.024 [0.015, 0.035] |
| Gain over nearest record | +0.098 | +0.101 |
| Hit rate in the model's top 10% | 48.4% | 40.2% |
| Random background | 47.0% | 36.5% |
| Nearest record | 41.3% | 29.3% |

- **Nearest record** is the simplest baseline: guess that a species occurs near where it was already recorded.
- **Brackets** are 95% intervals from the **spatial-block bootstrap**. We resample whole 0.3° blocks of survey cells (59 in Kerala, 49 in Taiwan) 1,000 times, because neighbouring cells are alike and resampling single cells would overstate certainty.
- The corrected model beats the uncorrected one for 76% of species in Kerala and 81% in Taiwan.
- The **effort-aware network** ties it in Taiwan. That network says a species is recorded with probability $\psi[1-(1-p)^U]$: occupancy $\psi$ times the chance of detecting it in $U$ visits. A pixel visited once and not recorded is weak evidence of absence; an unvisited pixel is no evidence.
- **Endemics** gain most in Kerala: 0.834 against 0.740 for nearest record.

**The point to land.** Among the 10% of unrecorded pairs the model ranks highest, the survey finds the species 48% of the time in Kerala against 27.5% at random, and 40% against 17% in Taiwan.

**Likely questions.**
- *What does +0.022 AUC mean?* It is modest on its own, but it is the difference random CV gets backwards, it is larger for endemics, and it doubles once doubtful atlas absences are removed. The hit rate is the practical number.
- *Isn't the atlas biased too?* Every cell was surveyed with a fixed effort. Its absences can be missed detections; modelling those made the gains larger.

## Slide 14. Who gains? (H8)

**What is on it.** Each point is a species and orange marks endemics. The y-axis is the AUC gain from target-group background.

**Kerala.**
- Endemics gain twice as much (+0.040 against +0.020).
- **Exposure** is the share of a species' atlas range that iNaturalist never visited. Once exposure enters the regression, the endemic effect disappears (+0.020 to +0.005, p = 0.52), and exposure itself is strongly positive.
- So in Kerala endemics gain because their ranges sit where nobody looks. This fits Botella et al. (2020): target-group background helps species whose ranges run against sampling effort.

**Taiwan.**
- Taiwan-concentrated species (at least 90% of their global iNaturalist records in Taiwan) also gain more (+0.033, permutation p = 0.0005).
- But they are *less* exposed (0.34 against 0.47), so the exposure explanation does not carry over. We present it as Kerala-specific.

**What replicates.** Elevation. Species with higher-elevation ranges gain more in both systems, about +0.02 to +0.03 AUC per km, even with exposure, training size and prevalence in the model. Mountains are where a random-background model most often confuses "rarely visited" with "unsuitable".

**Statistics on the slide.**
- The lines are simple one-variable fits. The quoted coefficients come from multiple regressions with HC3 robust standard errors, which stay valid when the spread of residuals is uneven.
- A **permutation test** shuffles the endemic labels many times and asks how often a gain this large appears by chance.

**The point to land.** We predicted Taiwan would replicate the exposure mechanism, and it did not. Reporting that honestly is why we trust the elevation result, which did replicate.

## Slide 15. A Bayesian model of sampling (H6)

**Why it exists.** The corrected richness maps did not predict where new iNaturalist records appeared. So we asked whether a model of the *sampling process* would.

**The model, for each 5 km cell.**
- The number of species living there is **Poisson** with mean $\Lambda$. Poisson is the standard distribution for counts of independent events.
- Each species has its own per-visit detection probability $p$, drawn from a **Beta(a, b)** distribution, a flexible distribution for numbers between 0 and 1. A few species are easy to see and most are hard.
- The chance a species is still undetected after $U$ visits:

$$P_0(U) = \frac{B(a, b+U)}{B(a, b)}$$

where $B$ is the beta function.
- New species found in $m$ more visits follow Poisson($\Lambda[P_0(U) - P_0(U+m)]$).
- The model extends Chiu's (2022) beta-binomial richness estimator to a spatial forecast.

**The Bayesian words.**
- **Prior:** a starting distribution on each coefficient that keeps estimates sensible where data are thin. The data turn it into a **posterior**.
- **MAP:** the single most probable parameter setting, which is what we fit.
- **Laplace approximation:** treats the posterior as a Gaussian around its peak to get uncertainty.
- **Log score:** the log of the probability the model gave to what actually happened, in "nats". Higher is better.

**Results.**
- It beats a negative-binomial effort regression at every forecast origin, by +0.020 to +0.054 nats per cell, with spatial-block intervals above zero.
- Classical Shen–Chao extrapolation scores −4 to −5, because it cannot forecast cells that were never sampled.
- **Ablation:** the intercepts-only version matches the full model. The gain comes from the detection-and-saturation structure: how much a cell has already been searched predicts discovery. Habitat covariates help only in ranking cells.
- The map is the deliverable: expected new records from the next ten visits to each cell.

**Limits.**
- It needs observer-level effort. On GeoLifeCLEF Europe, where a "visit" could only be defined as publisher × day, it lost to the regression.
- It forecasts new records; it does not estimate true missing richness.

**Likely question.** *Is this a new estimator?* No. The beta-binomial model is Chiu's. What we add is making it spatial and using it to forecast discovery.
